The game

Buried with the pharaohs, lost for four millennia

Mehen boards have turned up in Egyptian tombs dating back to roughly 3000 BCE — carved as a coiled serpent, its body divided into dozens of numbered chambers spiralling in toward a single space at the centre. No rulebook survived. For thousands of years, nobody has known exactly how it was played.

This app follows the marble-guessing ruleset reconstructed by James F. R. Masters in his 2024 paper for the Interdisciplinary Egyptology Journal — one educated reading of the evidence among several, and, as far as we know, its first playable digital form.

Masters, J.F.R. (2024). "Mehen: The Game of the Snake." Interdisciplinary Egyptology Journal, Vienna.

How to play

Along the serpent, four turns of fortune

Mehen isn't won by rolling well — it's won by guessing well. Every turn is a small act of bluffing, spiralling toward the same conclusion: whoever reaches Ra first, wins.

𓃭

The tail

Entering the board

A piece enters at the space equal to its owner's hidden marble count, minus one. Where you choose to hide sets where you begin.

𓁿

The coil

Hide, and guess

Secretly cup one to five marbles. Your opponent calls a number. Guess wrong, and the hider advances a piece; guess right, and the guesser takes the move — or unleashes something worse.

𓃰

The lion's pin

A challenge, not a roll

Win a guess, and you may pin an opponent's piece with a lion. It's frozen until its owner wins a 50/50 “which fist” challenge: they hide a single marble in one hand, and if the lion's owner picks the wrong hand, the beast is slain and the piece breaks free.

𓋹

Ra's sanctuary

The finish

First player to walk every one of their pieces from the tail to the centre space claims the game — and the favour of Ra.

Three ways to be outguessed
